A committee of parents from Millard Fillmore Elementary School has delivered a petition to the City Museum of Art demanding that all unclothed figures in its collection be covered, removed, or turned to face the wall before the spring field trips resume.

The petition, which bears three hundred signatures and one drawing of a horse, follows a fourth-grade visit to the Renaissance galleries last month. During that visit the class is reported to have stood for nearly two minutes before a reclining Venus, painted in 1538 and on public view, without incident, for the four hundred and eighty-eight years since.

Parents learned of the exposure that evening, when a boy of nine told his mother at supper that he had seen a painting of a lady and a small dog. Asked which he had liked better, he said the dog.

> “We are not against art. We simply ask that it be dressed.”
>
> The chairwoman of the Parents’ Committee on Appropriate Masterpieces

The Museum has offered a compromise. Under a pilot scheme now in effect, black bars have been applied to the works in question by a conservator, who is said to have wept throughout. The bars are removable, the Museum noted, and have been positioned in consultation with a panel of parents who examined each painting closely, and for some time, to be certain.

The Committee has called the measure a start. It has since submitted a second list, which includes eleven cherubs, a marble discus thrower, a bowl of fruit it describes as suggestive, and a skeleton in the natural history annex which it concedes is not nude in the usual sense but is, in its words, the logical conclusion.

A motion to extend the bars to the faces of the paintings, so that the figures could not see the children either, was carried by a show of hands.

Teachers at the school have expressed some puzzlement. One observed that every child on the trip carried a telephone on which the whole of human depravity may be summoned in under four seconds, and that none of them had thought to use it in the museum because the museum had no signal.

The Museum’s director said the institution takes the concerns of families seriously and has no wish to distress anyone. He asked only that the Committee stop bringing its own tape.

The children were not consulted. Several have asked when they may go back, and whether the gift shop still has the pencils.
